About Fair Value Ledger

A calculator that turns a company's trailing earnings and a growth rate into a "fair value" estimate. This page explains exactly what the app fetches, exactly how it calculates, and exactly what it doesn't do — including the caveats and known limitations, not just the marketing version.

What this app is — and isn't

Fair Value Ledger computes a classic EPS-growth valuation heuristic: a Rule #1-style discounted exit-multiple model. It starts from two inputs — a company's trailing twelve-month earnings per share (EPS TTM) and an assumed growth rate — and produces a single "fair value" price to compare against the current market price.

It is not a discounted cash-flow (DCF) model. Rule #1's method here projects one single future EPS figure and discounts it back once — it does not model a series of annual cash flows the way a real DCF does. It's also not a recommendation engine: no data source here can tell you what a stock is "really" worth, only what this one specific, simple formula produces for a given set of assumptions.

Where the numbers come from

Two independent, live data sources feed the calculation:

Every fetched figure is cached on disk for 24 hours to avoid hitting API rate limits on repeat lookups; a "Refresh live data" button bypasses the cache when you want the very latest numbers.

EPS (TTM) — how it's actually built

"EPS (TTM)" is not read as a single ready-made field from the API. It's built from the four most recent quarterly reports:

  1. Take each of the last 4 quarters' diluted EPS and diluted share count.
  2. Multiply each quarter's EPS by its share count to approximate that quarter's net income.
  3. Sum the four quarters' net income, then divide by the average diluted share count across those same four quarters.

This is deliberately GAAP diluted EPS — the figure reported in a company's actual financial statements, including one-time charges, stock-based compensation, and other items a company's own "adjusted" or "non-GAAP" EPS figure would exclude. GAAP is the more conservative choice for a valuation formula: it never quietly assumes away a real cost.

When the underlying data looks stale

Twelve Data's quarterly data has, in practice, been observed lagging a company's real earnings release by 10 or more days with no error or warning from the API — the endpoint simply keeps returning last quarter's numbers as if nothing changed. The app cross-checks for this: it compares the EPS-implied trailing P/E it just computed against Twelve Data's own separately-reported trailing P/E figure (a different, faster-updating part of their data). If the two disagree by more than 5%, the EPS is flagged as possibly stale.

When that happens, the app automatically tries Yahoo Finance's own trailing-EPS figure as a replacement — not just a warning. If Yahoo's figure is available, it's used instead, and the app labels the switch explicitly (both the ticker line at the top and the reference panel note where the number came from). If Yahoo is also unavailable, the app keeps Twelve Data's figure but marks it with a warning rather than presenting a possibly-wrong number silently.

Why not just splice in one fresh quarter from Yahoo? Yahoo's own per-quarter breakdown is either years out of date (a deprecated endpoint) or reports non-GAAP EPS, not GAAP — mixing that into an otherwise-GAAP calculation would be worse than either source alone. The app only ever swaps in Yahoo's whole trailing-twelve-month figure, never a single spliced-in quarter.

Growth rate — which one gets used, and why

The app has up to four different "growth rate" figures for a stock, and picks between them automatically in this priority order:

analyst 5-year estimate → 3-year historical CAGR → 1-year historical growth

The first one available wins. An analyst-aggregated 5-year estimate (from Yahoo, when available) is preferred because it's forward-looking; the 3-year CAGR is the usual fallback because it's resilient to a single volatile quarter (a one-time charge or gain in any one quarter gets diluted across a full calendar year on both ends of the comparison); the 1-year figure is the last resort, since it's the most exposed to one-off noise. All three historical figures compare full calendar years, not rolling twelve-month windows.

You can always override the growth rate manually in the form — the auto-selected value just seeds the field.

A separate, reference-only figure: EPS TTM growth (YoY)

The reference panel also shows "EPS TTM growth (YoY)" — the rolling twelve-month EPS figure compared against the rolling twelve-month figure exactly a year earlier. This is never fed into either valuation method; it's shown purely as supplementary color, because it answers a genuinely different question than the CAGR figures above ("how has the trailing year actually moved" vs. "how have full calendar years compared").

It requires 8 consecutive quarters of data. Twelve Data's plan tier used by this app caps quarterly history at 6, so this figure shows "n/a" for essentially every ticker today — that's an honest gap, not a bug. A shorter-window substitute was deliberately rejected: a real investigation (comparing this figure for a stock with a volatile one-time expense line) found that a 6-month-apart comparison can produce numbers wildly different from a genuine 12-month-apart one, depending purely on which one-time items happened to land inside vs. outside the shorter window. Showing "n/a" is safer than showing a number that looks like YoY growth but isn't.

Rule #1 (discounted exit multiple)

EPS(future) = EPS (TTM) × (1 + growth)^years Future Price = EPS(future) × Exit P/E multiple Fair Value = Future Price ÷ (1 + required return)^years

This projects EPS forward by compounding the growth rate for a chosen number of years, applies an assumed future P/E multiple to estimate a future stock price, then discounts that single future price back to today at a chosen required annual return.

This is explicitly not a full discounted cash-flow (DCF) model — it projects one single future EPS/price point and discounts it once, rather than modeling a series of annual cash flows.

Five inputs feed this method, each with its own default and its own validity check:

InputDefaultMeaning
Exit P/E multiple15Assumed P/E the stock trades at, N years from now
Required return15%Your target annualized return, used as the discount rate
Years10The projection horizon
Margin of Safety0% (site defaults to 25%)Extra discount applied to the computed "sticker price" before calling it your buy target

Because Rule #1 compounds a positive EPS at whatever rate it's given, a shrinking company (negative growth) still produces a sane, smaller positive fair value rather than a meaningless one.

Growth rate clamp

Whatever growth rate is used — auto-selected or manually entered — the method clamps it to a −5% floor internally before calculating (no maximum cap). The app always shows you both the raw figure and the clamped one it actually used.

Bear / Base / Bull scenarios (web only)

The site computes all three scenarios in parallel, each with its own independent Growth, Exit P/E, and Required Return inputs (Margin of Safety is shared across all three). The default assumptions, used only when a field is left blank:

ScenarioGrowth (if blank)Exit P/ERequired return
Bearbase growth × 0.75 (or −3 if base growth is negative)1015%
Basethe auto-selected or manually entered value1515%
Bullbase growth × 1.25 (or +3 if base growth is negative)2012%

The command-line tool only ever computes the Base scenario.

What's shown but never used in either calculation

Several figures appear on the page purely for context and play no role in either formula above:

  • Historical median P/E (1Y/3Y/5Y) — despite the label, these are medians (not averages) of one P/E point per fiscal year, chosen because a median is far less skewed by a single unusual year than a mean would be.
  • Trailing P/E & local PEG ratio — recomputed locally from price and the EPS actually used, never trusted from a data provider's own field.
  • Analyst price targets, recommendation, next-year growth estimate — from Yahoo, shown as market context.
  • Beta, P/S ratio, Rule of 40 — general financial-health reference figures, also from Yahoo.

Known limitations, stated plainly

  • Not a DCF — see "Rule #1" above.
  • GAAP-only EPS — no non-GAAP/adjusted figure is computed or offered as an alternative anywhere in the app.
  • "EPS TTM growth (YoY)" is n/a for essentially every ticker on this plan tier, since 8 quarters of history aren't available.
  • Stale-EPS detection only catches the specific pattern it was built to catch — Twelve Data's own trailing-P/E figure staying fresher than its income-statement data. A different kind of staleness (e.g. if that reference figure is stale too) would not be flagged.
  • Two independent live data providers means two independent points of failure and two different data-freshness cycles — the app degrades gracefully (cached data, or a labeled fallback) rather than failing outright, but it cannot guarantee both sources agree or are equally current at every moment.
